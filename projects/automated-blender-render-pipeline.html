The bottleneck

Every new skin and model had to be manually imported into Blender, have its materials assigned, and be re-rendered. Half a day of work, twice a week — and more when the team shipped a lot of new assets. Nobody had time to fix it. I saw it, proposed the fix to my lead, and got to work.

The shape of the fix

1
One .bat, one .blendA self-contained Blender automation pipeline. Export to the right folder with the right naming, double-click the .bat, come back to finished renders. No Blender skills required — any artist on the network can run it.
2
The chainIMPORT_ABC → ASSIGN_MATERIAL → IMPORT_TEXTURES → RENDER (stills + sequence) → shared folder.
3
Any PC on the network with Blender installedNo licensing, no server, no build step for the artists to think about.

Why it's stable: regenerate, don't patch

My first version tried to update hand-built shaders with hardcoded values. It worked, until it didn't — too fragile and unpredictable. The fix was to stop patching and start regenerating: every batch rebuilds the shaders from zero. Same input, same result, every time. Deterministic by design.

That single change is the difference between a script that works on your machine and a tool a studio can rely on.

Adopted, not just delivered

I defined the naming convention, coached the team, and presented the tool. Three days later I came back from a morning medical appointment to find the artists already running it on their own, without asking.

That's when I knew it was real: it gave the team an autonomy they needed without even knowing it. The tool is still in place today.
